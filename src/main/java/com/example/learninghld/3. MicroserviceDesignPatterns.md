### Disadvantages of Monolithic Architecture

- Tight Coupling
    - Changing one line can impact other components
    - Need to test/deploy entire application for one change
    - *Real-world analogy:* Like changing a single wire in a complex circuit board - you need to power down the entire system
    - *Example:* Updating a payment processing module requires testing the entire e-commerce platform
- Difficult to scale
    - If one component needs scaling, entire application needs to be scaled
    - *Resource inefficiency:* Underutilized resources for components that don't need scaling
    - *Example:* If only your payment processing needs more resources during sales, you still need to scale the entire application
- Expensive deployments and rollbacks
    - Entire application needs redeployment for a small change
    - *Increased risk:* Higher chance of deployment failures affecting the entire system
    - *Longer downtime:* Deployments take longer, increasing potential business impact
- Large codebase
    - Everything in a single application
    - Codebase grows large over time
    - Difficult to make changes, understand impact
    - *Onboarding challenges:* New developers take longer to understand the system
    - *Technology constraints:* Difficult to adopt new technologies or frameworks
- *Additional disadvantages:*
    - *Technology stack limitations:* Entire application must use the same language/framework
    - *Team coordination overhead:* Multiple teams working on the same codebase require significant coordination
    - *Single point of failure:* Issues in one component can bring down the entire application

### Why Microservices?

- To overcome monolithic disadvantages
- Split large application into small services
- *Definition:* An architectural style that structures an application as a collection of loosely coupled, independently deployable services
- *Historical context:* Evolved from Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA) but with more emphasis on fine-grained services and lightweight communication
- *Industry adoption:* Companies like Netflix, Amazon, Uber, and Spotify have successfully implemented microservices at scale
- *Core principle:* "Do one thing and do it well" - each service focuses on a specific business capability

### Advantages of Microservices

- Better separation of concerns
    - *Clear boundaries:* Each service has a well-defined responsibility
    - *Focused teams:* Teams can specialize in specific business domains
    - *Example:* Payment team focuses solely on payment processing, not on inventory management
- Loose coupling between services
    - *Independent evolution:* Services can evolve independently without affecting others
    - *Technology diversity:* Different services can use different technologies best suited for their specific needs
    - *Example:* Payment service in Java, recommendation engine in Python, frontend in JavaScript
- Independent deployment of services
    - *Reduced risk:* Smaller, focused deployments reduce the scope of potential failures
    - *Continuous delivery:* Services can be deployed independently, enabling more frequent releases
    - *Example:* Deploying an updated payment service without touching the catalog service
- Easy to scale out specific services
    - *Targeted scaling:* Scale only what needs scaling based on demand
    - *Resource efficiency:* Optimize resource allocation for specific services
    - *Example:* During holiday sales, scale only the checkout and payment services
- Faster release cycles
    - *Smaller codebases:* Easier to understand, test, and deploy
    - *Parallel development:* Multiple teams can work on different services simultaneously
    - *Reduced dependencies:* Teams can release without waiting for other teams
- *Additional advantages:*
    - *Fault isolation:* Failures in one service don't necessarily affect others
    - *Easier experimentation:* New technologies or approaches can be tried in individual services
    - *Team autonomy:* Teams can make decisions independently for their services
    - *Better alignment with business:* Services map to business capabilities, making it easier to align technical and business priorities

### Disadvantages of Microservices

- Proper service boundaries/decomposition is challenging
    - *Domain knowledge required:* Requires deep understanding of the business domain
    - *Premature decomposition risks:* Breaking down services too early can lead to wrong boundaries
    - *Evolution challenges:* Service boundaries may need to change as business evolves
    - *Example:* Deciding whether "order" and "payment" should be separate services or combined
- Inter-service communication is complex
    - Monitoring calls across services
    - Handling failures
    - *Network reliability issues:* Services communicate over networks which can fail
    - *Latency concerns:* Multiple service calls can increase response times
    - *Versioning challenges:* Managing API changes across services
    - *Example:* An order service needs to communicate with inventory, payment, and shipping services
- Distributed transaction management is difficult
    - Across multiple databases
    - *Consistency challenges:* Maintaining data consistency across services
    - *Eventual consistency:* Often need to adopt eventual consistency models
    - *Saga pattern:* Need to implement complex compensation mechanisms
    - *Example:* Ensuring an order is only confirmed when payment is successful and inventory is updated
- *Additional disadvantages:*
    - *Operational complexity:* More moving parts to monitor, deploy, and maintain
    - *Debugging challenges:* Tracing issues across multiple services is harder
    - *Testing complexity:* Integration testing across services requires more sophisticated approaches
    - *Infrastructure overhead:* Requires robust service discovery, load balancing, and API gateway solutions
    - *Team coordination:* While reduced, some coordination is still needed, especially for cross-cutting changes

### Microservices Design Phases

- Decomposition patterns
    - *Purpose:* Determining how to break down the application into services
    - *Key question:* "What should be a microservice?"
    - *Challenge:* Finding the right granularity and boundaries
- Database patterns
    - *Purpose:* Managing data across multiple services
    - *Key question:* "How should data be stored and accessed?"
    - *Challenge:* Maintaining data consistency across services
- Communication patterns
    - *Purpose:* Enabling services to interact with each other
    - *Key question:* "How should services communicate?"
    - *Challenge:* Ensuring reliable and efficient communication
- Integration patterns
    - *Purpose:* Connecting services with external systems and each other
    - *Key question:* "How should services be integrated?"
    - *Challenge:* Managing dependencies and versioning
- Deployment patterns
    - *Purpose:* Efficiently deploying and managing services
    - *Key question:* "How should services be deployed?"
    - *Challenge:* Automating deployment and ensuring reliability
- Cross-cutting concerns like monitoring, logging
    - *Purpose:* Maintaining visibility and operational control
    - *Key question:* "How to monitor and troubleshoot the system?"
    - *Challenge:* Implementing consistent observability across services

### Decomposition Patterns

- By business capability
    - Split based on business functions like order mgmt, inventory, etc
    - *Definition:* Organizing services around business capabilities rather than technical functions
    - *Advantages:* Aligns with business organization, reduces cross-team dependencies
    - *Example:* E-commerce platform with separate services for product catalog, shopping cart, order management, customer accounts, and payments
    - *Implementation approach:* Start by mapping business capabilities, then define service boundaries
- By subdomain - Domain Driven Design (DDD)
    - Split large domains into multiple services
        - E.g. splitting payment domain
    - *Definition:* Using DDD principles to identify bounded contexts and subdomains
    - *Key concepts:*
        - *Bounded Context:* A boundary within which a particular domain model applies
        - *Ubiquitous Language:* Common language used by all team members within a bounded context
        - *Context Mapping:* Defining relationships between different bounded contexts
    - *Example:* Breaking down a payment domain into payment processing, refunds, payment methods, and fraud detection services
    - *Implementation approach:* Conduct domain modeling workshops with domain experts
- *Additional decomposition patterns:*
    - *By transaction:* Group operations that are part of the same transaction
    - *By volatility:* Separate components that change frequently from stable ones
    - *Strangler pattern:* Gradually migrate from monolith to microservices
    - *Sidecar pattern:* Attach supporting services to a main service
- three important patterns:
  - *Strangler Pattern:* Used for *refactoring* a monolithic service into microservices.
  - *Saga Pattern:* Solves the problem of *distributed transactions* across multiple databases.
  - *CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation):* Separates command (write) and query (read) operations for improved performance and scalability.

### *Microservice Design Patterns Overview:*

- *Definition:* Design patterns in microservices are proven solutions to common architectural challenges in distributed systems.
- *Importance:* These patterns help address the inherent complexities of microservice architectures.
- *Categories:* Microservice patterns can be categorized into decomposition, data management, communication, deployment, and operational patterns.
- *Evolution:* Many patterns have evolved from enterprise integration patterns and distributed systems best practices.

### *Strangler Pattern:*

- *Purpose:* Gradually refactoring a monolithic application into microservices.
- *Origin:* Named after the strangler fig, a vine that gradually envelops and replaces its host tree.
- *How it Works:*
  - A "controller" is introduced to handle requests.
  - Initially, the controller forwards all traffic to the monolithic application.
  - Gradually, specific functionalities are extracted into microservices, and the controller routes traffic to them.
  - As more functionalities are migrated, the controller forwards less traffic to the monolithic application, eventually strangling it.
- *Advantages:*
  - Minimizes disruption to existing services.
  - Allows for a gradual transition to microservices.
  - Reduces risk by enabling incremental migration and testing.
  - Provides immediate business value with each migrated component.
- *Implementation Considerations:*
  - Start with less critical, more isolated components.
  - Use feature flags to control traffic routing during migration.
  - Implement robust monitoring to compare behavior between old and new implementations.
  - Consider data migration strategies alongside functionality migration.
- *Real-world Application:*
  - Netflix used the Strangler Pattern to migrate from a monolithic DVD rental system to a microservices-based streaming platform.
  - Amazon transformed its monolithic retail platform into hundreds of microservices over several years.
- *Example:*
  - Imagine a monolithic e-commerce website being refactored into microservices.
  - The controller initially directs all traffic to the monolithic website.
  - Gradually, functionalities like order placement, inventory management, and payment processing are moved to individual microservices.
  - The controller gradually routes more traffic to these microservices, eventually reducing the reliance on the monolithic application.
- *Visual Analogy:* Think of renovating a house while still living in it. You don't demolish everything at once; instead, you renovate one room at a time, ensuring the house remains functional throughout the process.

### *Data Management in Microservices:*

- *Two Approaches:*
  - *Database for Each Individual Service:* Each microservice has its own dedicated database, promoting autonomy and isolation.
  - *Shared Database:* All microservices share a single database, simplifying data access but potentially leading to complexities.
- *Why Database per Service is Preferred:*
  - *Scalability:* Allows for independent scaling of individual services without impacting others.
  - *Isolation:* Changes in one service's database don't affect others.
  - *Technology Flexibility:* Services can choose different databases based on their specific needs.
  - *Resilience:* Failures in one database don't affect other services.
  - *Ownership clarity:* Each team has clear ownership of their data.
  - *Reduced contention:* No competition for database resources between services.
- *Implementation Challenges of Database per Service:*
  - *Data consistency:* Maintaining consistency across multiple databases.
  - *Distributed transactions:* Handling operations that span multiple services.
  - *Data duplication:* Some data may need to be replicated across services.
  - *Query complexity:* Joining data across services becomes more difficult.
- *Advantages of Shared Database*
  - Join Query: Can easily perform complex queries across related data.
  - Transactional Property (ACID): Simpler to maintain data integrity with traditional transactions.
  - Simpler initial implementation: Easier to migrate from a monolithic application.
  - Reduced operational overhead: Fewer databases to manage and monitor.
- *Drawbacks of Shared Database:*
  - *Performance Bottlenecks:* Increased contention and performance issues as more services access the same database.
  - *Complexity:* Managing dependencies and ensuring consistency across multiple services becomes difficult.
  - *Limited Scalability:* Scaling the entire database is necessary, even if only one service needs more resources.
  - *Schema coupling:* Changes to the schema can affect multiple services.
  - *Deployment coordination:* Database changes require coordination across teams.
- *Hybrid Approaches:*
  - *Database per service group:* Related services share a database while maintaining separation from other groups.
  - *Polyglot persistence:* Using different database types for different services based on their data requirements.
  - *CQRS with event sourcing:* Using event logs as the source of truth with specialized read models.
- *Data Consistency Patterns:*
  - *Two-Phase Commit (2PC):* Traditional approach for distributed transactions, but with performance limitations.
  - *Saga Pattern:* Breaking transactions into a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions.
  - *Eventual Consistency:* Accepting that data will be consistent over time, not immediately.
- *Real-world Example:*
  - Amazon's microservices each own their data and API, with no direct access to another service's database.
  - Netflix uses Cassandra for its distributed nature, with each service managing its own data domain.

### *Saga Pattern:*

- *Purpose:* Managing distributed transactions across multiple databases, ensuring data consistency even if some operations fail.
- *Problem it Solves:* In microservices, traditional ACID transactions across multiple services aren't feasible, yet business operations often span multiple services.
- *Origin:* Concept introduced in a 1987 paper by Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem, adapted for microservices.
- *How it Works:*
  - A sequence of local transactions is executed within each participating microservice.
  - Each transaction updates the database and publishes an event.
  - Subsequent transactions listen to these events and continue the process.
  - In case of failure, compensation events are published to undo completed operations and maintain consistency.
- *Types of Sagas:*
  - *Choreography:* Each service manages its own transactions and listens to events from other services.
    - *Advantages:* Decentralized, less coupling, easier to extend
    - *Disadvantages:* Harder to track, potential for cyclic dependencies
    - *Best for:* Simpler workflows with fewer steps
  - *Orchestration:* A centralized orchestrator manages the transaction flow and handles compensation logic.
    - *Advantages:* Centralized control, easier to track and monitor
    - *Disadvantages:* Potential single point of failure, higher coupling
    - *Best for:* Complex workflows with many steps or participants
- *Implementation Technologies:*
  - *Message Brokers:* Kafka, RabbitMQ, Amazon SQS
  - *Workflow Engines:* Camunda, Zeebe, Netflix Conductor
  - *Custom Solutions:* Purpose-built saga coordinators
- *Example:*
  - An order processing saga involving services for order creation, inventory management, and payment processing.
  - If the payment service fails, compensation events are triggered to cancel the order and update inventory.
- *Detailed Example Flow:*
  1. Order Service: Creates order in "pending" state → Emits "OrderCreated" event
  2. Inventory Service: Reserves inventory → Emits "InventoryReserved" event
  3. Payment Service: Processes payment → Emits "PaymentCompleted" event
  4. Order Service: Updates order to "confirmed" → Emits "OrderConfirmed" event
  5. If Payment fails → Emits "PaymentFailed" event
  6. Inventory Service: Releases reserved inventory → Emits "InventoryReleased" event
  7. Order Service: Updates order to "cancelled"
- *Advantages:*
  - Guarantees data consistency in distributed systems.
  - Provides a mechanism for handling failures and rollbacks.
  - Allows for flexibility in service interactions.
  - Enables long-running transactions that would be impractical with traditional approaches.
- *Disadvantages:*
  - Increased complexity compared to local transactions.
  - Requires careful design and implementation to ensure correctness.
  - Eventual consistency model may not be suitable for all use cases.
  - Debugging and monitoring can be challenging.
- *Real-world Applications:*
  - E-commerce order processing
  - Travel booking systems (flights, hotels, car rentals)
  - Financial transaction processing
- *Visual Analogy:* Think of a relay race where each runner (service) completes their leg and passes the baton (event). If a runner drops the baton, everyone returns to their starting positions (compensation).

### *CQRS Pattern:*

- *Purpose:* Separating read (query) operations from write (command) operations for better performance and scalability.
- *Name Origin:* CQRS stands for Command Query Responsibility Segregation, a term coined by Greg Young.
- *Problem it Solves:* In complex systems, the data model for reading data efficiently often differs from the model optimized for writing data.
- *How it Works:*
  - The system maintains separate models for read and write operations.
  - Write operations are performed through commands, updating the write model.
  - Read operations access the read model, which can be optimized for fast retrieval.
  - Changes to the write model are propagated to the read model, often asynchronously.
- *Key Components:*
  - *Commands:* Represent intentions to change the system state (e.g., "CreateOrder", "UpdateCustomerAddress")
  - *Command Handlers:* Process commands and update the write model
  - *Events:* Represent facts about changes that have occurred
  - *Event Handlers:* Update the read model based on events
  - *Queries:* Requests for information from the read model
  - *Query Handlers:* Retrieve and return data from the read model
- *Levels of Implementation:*
  - *Basic CQRS:* Separate models within the same service and database
  - *Advanced CQRS:* Separate services and databases for reads and writes
  - *CQRS with Event Sourcing:* Store events as the source of truth instead of current state
- *Advantages:*
  - *Performance Improvement:* Optimized read models can handle queries more efficiently.
  - *Scalability:* Read and write models can be scaled independently based on their specific needs.
  - *Flexibility:* Allows for different data structures and query languages for read and write operations.
  - *Optimized Data Models:* Each model can be designed for its specific purpose without compromise.
  - *Separation of Concerns:* Clearer code organization and responsibility boundaries.
  - *Improved Security:* Can implement different security models for reads vs. writes.
- *Example:*
  - A blog application where write operations are performed on a relational database, while read operations access a denormalized view optimized for fast search.
- *Detailed Example:*
  - *Write Model:* Normalized SQL database storing articles, authors, categories, and tags in separate tables
  - *Read Model:* Denormalized NoSQL document store with articles pre-joined with author information and tags
  - *Synchronization:* When an article is published (command), an event is emitted that updates the read model
  - *Result:* Complex queries like "find all articles by author X with tag Y" are much faster on the read model
- *Challenges:*
  - Maintaining consistency between the read and write models.
  - Ensuring the read model is up-to-date with changes in the write model.
  - Increased complexity in the overall system architecture.
  - Potential for eventual consistency issues that must be handled.
  - Learning curve for developers unfamiliar with the pattern.
- *When to Use:*
  - Complex domains with different read and write requirements
  - Systems with significant disparity between read and write operations (e.g., many more reads than writes)
  - Applications requiring high performance for both reads and writes
  - When different teams are responsible for different aspects of the system
- *When Not to Use:*
  - Simple CRUD applications where read and write models would be nearly identical
  - Systems where immediate consistency is an absolute requirement
  - Small applications where the added complexity outweighs the benefits
- *Real-world Applications:*
  - E-commerce product catalogs (complex writes, high-volume reads)
  - Financial reporting systems (complex aggregations for reads)
  - Social media platforms (high write volume, complex read queries)
- *Visual Analogy:* Think of a restaurant with separate preparation and serving areas. The kitchen (write model) is organized for efficient food preparation, while the dining area (read model) is optimized for customer experience. The waitstaff (event handlers) ensure that food prepared in the kitchen reaches the correct tables.
